🎬 Video Prompt Writing Guide

T2VA · I2VA · FL2VA · L2VA Full‑Reference Mode 🧒 Kid‑Friendly Edition

📌 Part 1: Base Generation Modes (T2VA / I2VA / FL2VA / L2VA)

1.1 Task Overview

T2VAText → Full audiovisual timeline
I2VAFirst‑frame instruction + forward visual path
FL2VAFirst‑and‑last‑frame instruction + continuous path
L2VALast‑frame instruction + convergence to last frame
🧒
5‑year‑old explanation: These acronyms are four different ways to “make a movie.”
• T2VA: You tell the AI a story, and it draws the pictures and adds the sound.
• I2VA: You give it a “first picture,” and it keeps going from there.
• FL2VA: You give it the “first” and “last” pictures, and it connects the middle.
• L2VA: You give it only the “last” picture, and it works backward to figure out the beginning.

1.2 Final Prompt Structure

Instruction

  • T2VA: No image alignment; start directly with the three core fields.
  • I2VA:
    For the target video, at 0.00 seconds of the target video, fully reference <Picture 1> (from [Shot 1]).
  • FL2VA:
    Reference image alignment to target video — Figure 1 (from Shot 1) aligns to 0.00s; Figure 2 (from Shot N) aligns to S.SS seconds.
  • L2VA:
    Reference image alignment to target video — <Picture 1> (from [Shot N]) aligns to S.SS seconds.

Three Core Fields

integrated_multimodal_description: [Shot 1] …… overall_soundscape: …… non_diegetic_music: ……
  • integrated_multimodal_description: Visuals, action, camera, speakers, dialogue, singing, and diegetic sound along the timeline.
  • overall_soundscape: Ambient sound, physical action effects, non‑verbal vocalisations.
  • non_diegetic_music: Background music heard only by the audience.
🧒
5‑year‑old explanation: These three fields split your “movie script” into three parts:
• First: what happens in the picture — like telling a story.
• Second: environmental sounds — rain, footsteps, etc.
• Third: background music — only the audience hears it, not the characters.

1.3 Keyframe Integration

I2VA: Forward development from an image

<Picture 1> is the actual first frame at 0.00s. The description should first establish the style, subject, composition, and scene anchor from the image, then describe subsequent actions. Character identity, clothing, colours, key objects, and spatial relationships should remain consistent.

Recommended structure First‑frame anchor → action onset → continuous development → outcome or reaction

FL2VA: Describe the path between first and last frames

Figure 1 is the start, Figure 2 is the end. Focus on subject movement, pose changes, object manipulation, composition evolution, and scene/lighting transitions. FL2VA often favours a single shot for interpolation. The final frame must be reached by the last [Shot N] at the video's end.

Recommended structure First‑frame state → observable intermediate changes → differences gradually narrow → final‑frame state

L2VA: Infer the beginning, land on the ending image

<Picture 1> is the last frame, belonging to the final [Shot N]. Infer a reasonable earlier state based on user intent and the final frame, then describe how it gradually approaches the reference image.

Recommended structure Reasonable prior state → clear action and transition path → gradual convergence in the last shot → land on the final frame

🧒
5‑year‑old explanation: “Keyframes” are the most important “photos” in a movie.
• I2VA: You give a “first photo,” and the AI keeps telling the story.
• FL2VA: You give a “first” and a “last” photo, and the AI imagines the middle.
• L2VA: You give only the “last” photo, and the AI guesses the beginning, then plays to the end.

1.4 Core Writing Rules (shared)

① Multimodal description along the timeline

integrated_multimodal_description is the main body. Every detail corresponds to visible or audible content: visual style, initial composition, subject appearance and position, scene and key props, action and reactions, camera cuts, spoken content, and synchronous diegetic sound.

At the start of [Shot 1], state the overall style and initial composition. Common styles: Cinematiclive-action2D-animated3D CGclaymationwatercolorvintage film.

[Shot 1] Live‑action, cinematic feel, a medium‑wide shot frames ……

② Shots and cuts

No timestamp for the first shot. Subsequent shots use consecutive numbering and start with a strictly increasing cut time:

[Shot 2] At 00:03.500, the cut goes to ……

Use “cut to” for ordinary transitions; only use dissolves, fades, or wipes if explicitly requested. If only a change in distance or slight angle is needed, prefer camera movement over a cut.

③ Camera movement: type + magnitude + speed

Write camera movement as part of the natural action description:

The lens zooms in slowly with small magnitude on the letter she is folding. The lens pans right quickly with large magnitude, revealing the open doorway. The camera remains fixed as the runner exits the frame.

④ Speakers, dialogue, and singing

Use stable IDs (S1), (S2). For multiple speakers together, use (S1,S2). When a speaker first appears, provide enough visual and audio context. Identity phrases, IDs, actions, and delivery style go outside <d>; inside <d> put only the language tag and the actual spoken content, transcribed verbatim.

A young woman with a soft, slightly breathy voice (S1) says: <d>[English] I get off at the next station.</d> Two children (S1,S2) call out together: <d>[English] Wait for us!</d>

Use “says off‑screen” for voice‑over, and add a note that lips remain closed after each <d> block.

For dialogue that continues across cuts, use <scenetrans>; for cut‑off speech, use <cutoff>.

⑤ On‑screen text

Place visible on‑screen text in double quotes, transcribed verbatim.

A red neon sign reading “Open” glows above the doorway.

⑥ overall_soundscape

Summarise ambient sound, physical action effects, and non‑verbal vocalisations in 1‑4 sentences. Do not repeat dialogue, singing, or diegetic music here. Use N/A only for complete silence.

overall_soundscape: Steady rain taps against the café windows, with a low interior ambient hum persisting throughout. The entry doorbell rings once, followed by wet footsteps and the faint scrape of a chair.

⑦ non_diegetic_music

Describe the background music the characters cannot hear in 1‑3 sentences, focusing on instrumentation, tempo, rhythm, and dynamic changes. Avoid abstract emotional terms. Use N/A if absent.

non_diegetic_music: Sparse piano notes emerge at a slow tempo, accompanied by sustained low strings that gradually swell and fade.
🧒
5‑year‑old explanation: These writing rules are like a “good‑word template” for your essay:
• Camera movement = how the camera moves — zoom, pan, etc.
• Speakers = who is talking — give each person a number so you don't mix them up.
• On‑screen text = words you see on the screen — write them exactly as they appear.
• Soundscape & music = background sounds and background music — describe them separately.

1.5 Complete Example

T2VA

integrated_multimodal_description: [Shot 1] Live‑action, cinematic feel, a medium‑wide shot frames a baker opening the roll‑up door of a small street‑side bakery just before sunrise. The lens zooms in slowly with small magnitude, and the middle‑aged baker, with a calm, slightly hoarse voice (S1), places a fresh loaf on the wooden counter and says: <d>[English] First batch of the morning.</d> [Shot 2] At 00:05.000, the cut goes to a close‑up of the sliced bread steaming, with the baker's last words continuing from the previous shot. overall_soundscape: The wooden roll‑up door scrapes open on the quiet street, trays clink softly inside the bakery. The doorbell rings once, followed by brisk footsteps and the crisp sound of bread being sliced. non_diegetic_music: Gentle acoustic guitar plays at a moderate tempo, accompanied by sparse double‑bass notes, fading out softly at the end.
🧒
5‑year‑old explanation: These examples are like “model essays.” Once you understand one, you get them all. Remember the three parts:
• Story & visuals (integrated...)
• Environmental sounds (overall...)
• Background music (non...)

🔁 Part 2: Full‑Reference Mode

🧒
5‑year‑old explanation: “Full‑reference mode” means you show the AI some “reference materials” (pictures, videos, audio) and ask it to generate a new video that follows those materials. It's like building with LEGO using the instruction booklet.

2.1 Overall Structure & Output Fields

The full‑reference output consists of these six parts in order:

PartPurpose
subject_definitionsDefine referenced content and their reference tags
summarySummarise task type, target video, and key reference relationships
retention_analysisDescribe how referenced content is preserved, migrated, or reused
detailed_descriptionDescribe visuals, action, camera, sound, and dialogue in playback order
overall_soundscapeSummarise environmental and physical sounds
non_diegetic_musicDescribe background music heard only by the audience
🧒
5‑year‑old explanation: These six parts are like an “essay outline”:
1. List what you're referencing (definitions).
2. Summarise what you're making (summary).
3. Analyse what's kept and what's changed (retention analysis).
4. Write the detailed story (detailed description).
5‑6. Finally, write the environmental sounds and background music.

2.2 Reference Tag Definitions

<Subject N> A visible entity (character, scene, costume, action, style, etc.) abstracted from reference material that can be reused or modified in the target video <Picture N> A reference image used as a concrete target frame or shot‑planning anchor <Video N> A reference video providing source footage, a continuation start point, or an entire video time structure <Audio N> An audio signal that is copied or referenced

Once assigned, a reference tag retains the same meaning across all six parts. subject_definitions must define every referenced piece of content that must be tracked separately.

🧒
5‑year‑old explanation: “Reference tags” are like giving nicknames to your reference materials:
• <Subject> = “character” — a person, animal, or thing.
• <Picture> = “photo” — a single image.
• <Video> = “video” — a video clip.
• <Audio> = “sound” — an audio clip.
When you write the prompt later, use these nicknames instead of the long descriptions.

<Subject N> Example

<Subject 1> is the young woman from <Picture 1>, with long dark hair, wearing a blue cardigan and a thin silver necklace.

<Picture N> Example

<Picture 2> is the first frame of [Shot 1], showing a woman seated by a café window.

<Video N> Example

<Video 1> is the source video used for the target video edit.

<Audio N> Example

<Audio 1> is the vocal‑timbre reference for <Subject 1> (S1).

2.3 Summary & Retention Analysis

Summary (summary)

Prefix with the task type in square brackets:

[reference generation] …… [video editing + reference generation + audio reuse] ……
Task TypeUse Case
keyframe completionImage as first frame, keyframe, last frame, etc.
reference generationProvide generative guidance for character, scene, style, etc., without serving as a specific frame
video editingDirect modification of an existing source video
video continuationNew content extends from an existing source video
audio reuseSame audio signal fully or partially reused
audio referenceReferenced music style, timbre, spoken content, etc., without copying the signal

Retention Analysis (retention_analysis) — Complete Tag Guide

What it does: It tells the AI, for each reference you provide (image/video/audio), what must stay exactly the same and what can be changed. Without it, the AI might arbitrarily alter your favourite character's appearance or ignore the reference style entirely.

Tag Meaning 🧒 5‑year‑old explanation 💡 Example
fully_preserved Fully preserved “This must be exactly the same — no changes!” Subject 1 is defined as “woman in a blue cardigan” and tagged fully_preserved. The AI must keep the blue cardigan; red won't do.
partially_preserved Partially preserved “You can change this a little, but keep some of the original character.” The subject is “long hair” and tagged partially_preserved. The AI could style it as a “ponytail,” but the length attribute remains.
attribute_transfer Attribute transfer “Take a feature from this thing and give it to another thing.” A reference video shows a “cat jumping.” Tagged attribute_transfer, the AI can make your “dog” perform that jump — the action is preserved, but the actor changes.
weak_reference Weak reference “Just go with the vibe — details don't have to match exactly.” A reference image is a “vintage café.” The AI only needs to generate a café with a “similar retro feel”; the wall art and table arrangement can vary.

🎵 Audio‑specific tags

Tag Meaning 🧒 5‑year‑old explanation 💡 Example
fully_copy Full copy “Copy this sound exactly as it is.” Place the reference audio file as the target video's complete sound track with no modifications.
partially_copy Partial copy “Only copy part of it; add the rest myself.” Copy only the “rain” layer from the reference audio, but re‑do the “footsteps” and “background music” yourself.
reference Reference (timbre/style) “Mimic this person's voice for new lines.” Reference a voice actor's timbre and pacing, but have the AI speak entirely new dialogue instead of copying the original.
weak_reference Weak reference “Just go for that kind of feel — doesn't have to match exactly.” Reference an “upbeat pop track” as a style direction; the AI composes a new piece with a similar mood.
🧒
5‑year‑old summary: “Retention analysis” is the AI's “instruction manual” — it tells the AI which references to copy exactly and which to freely interpret.
• fully_preserved = must be identical.
• partially_preserved = can tweak a little.
• attribute_transfer = lend a feature to something else.
• weak_reference / reference = follow the general feel; details are flexible.

📖 Real‑world example (from the guide's “complete example”)

<Subject 1> (appears in [Shot 1], [Shot 2], [Shot 3]): fully_preserved — exposed brick walls, orange tufted sofa, patterned cushions, neon sign, and wooden coffee table are all retained.

<Audio 1>: reference — its vocal timbre guides the delivery of <Subject 3>'s dialogue, but the original signal is not copied.

🔍 Breakdown:
• For Subject 1 (café environment), tagged fully_preserved. No matter how the shots cut (all 3 shots include it), the red‑brick walls, orange sofa, and neon sign must appear unchanged in every frame. This is the story's fixed stage.
• For Audio 1 (reference voice), tagged reference. The AI cannot copy the original recording's exact words; it must mimic the person's timbre, pace, and tone. The AI generates new sentences (e.g., dialogue) that sound like the same person speaking.

🤔 Why it matters

Without retention_analysis, the AI might:

  • Turn your “red‑brick café” into a random “blue‑walled diner.”
  • Match your “deep‑voiced middle‑aged man” with a “high‑pitched cartoon voice.”

With it, the AI strictly follows your creative intent — core elements (fully_preserved) never change, while non‑core elements (weak_reference / reference) are free to evolve. It's like giving the AI a precise GPS so it generates results that are both faithful to the references and creative.

2.4 Detailed Description (detailed_description)

Basic format follows the same rules as Part 1, with these full‑reference differences:

AspectT2VAFull‑Reference Mode
Main fieldintegrated_multimodal_descriptiondetailed_description
Style openingWritten after [Shot 1]Established in one or two sentences before [Shot 1]
Reference infoNo full‑reference tagsInsert <Subject N>, <Picture N>, <Video N>, <Audio N> on first appearance and where relevant
Audio relationshipsDescribes the target video's own soundReference <Audio N> in the corresponding shot or audio section, stating signal copy or reference

Speakers & audio sources: When a referenced subject actually speaks, retain both the visual reference tag and the speaker ID:

<Subject 2> (S1) turns to the woman and says: <d>[English] Last summer, I went to my grandfather's house. He talked about you.</d>

If the spoken content is merely a lyric prompt reused from background music, and no character actually produces that sound, use <Audio N> as the audible source and do not invent a (Sx).

🧒
5‑year‑old explanation: Full‑reference “detailed description” is similar to the normal mode, but you add reference tags.
Whenever a character, picture, video, or sound appears, mark it with its nickname (tag), just like adding a footnote that says “this part references that picture.”
